A PRACTICAL GUIDE FOR LIFE IN THE U.S.

Money steps for bills, credit, and a tighter budget

If money feels tight, begin by seeing what is due and what help may be available before choosing the next action. The steps below cover bills, credit, savings, and financial assistance without assuming every program fits everyone.

This page offers general information. Services and rules can vary by state and change over time. Check the sources and ask a qualified professional about your own medical, legal, or financial situation.

14 STEPS

Steps you can take

14 steps to explore. Choose one that fits your situation; each step has a source you can check.

01

Put every bill on one calendar

#

Seeing amounts and due dates together helps you plan the month.

First step

Write each bill, amount, and due date on paper or in your phone; check the list once a week.

Source

  • CFPB: Bill calendar
02

If money is short, rank the bills

#

Different missed payments have different consequences.

First step

List what is due and the effect of paying late. Protect essentials such as housing, utilities, food, and what you need to earn income.

Source

  • CFPB: Prioritizing bills
03

Call before a credit card payment is missed

#

The card company may discuss a payment change if you are in trouble.

First step

Explain what happened, what you can afford, and when you could resume normal payments; ask for the agreement in writing.

Source

  • CFPB: When you cannot pay a credit card bill
04

Start an emergency fund at any size

#

Even a small reserve can help with an unexpected bill.

First step

Choose a safe, accessible place for savings and set aside an amount that fits your situation when you can.

Source

  • CFPB: Building an emergency fund
05

Check your credit reports for errors

#

A report can reveal accounts or details you do not recognize.

First step

Use the official AnnualCreditReport.com link from the CFPB and review each report.

Source

  • CFPB: Free credit reports
06

Consider a free credit freeze

#

It can make it harder for someone else to open new credit in your name.

First step

If it fits your situation, contact all three credit bureaus. You can lift the freeze when you need to apply for credit.

Source

  • FTC: Credit freezes and fraud alerts
07

Verify a surprise request for money

#

Scammers create urgency and impersonate people or agencies.

First step

Pause. Contact the person or organization using a number or website you found yourself, never the one in the message.

Source

  • FTC: Signs of a scam
08

Check what your bank account costs

#

Monthly and transaction fees can eat into money you need.

First step

Read your account fee schedule and compare it with another bank or credit union account that fits how you use it.

Source

  • CFPB: Compare checking account fees
09

Recheck withholding after a life change

#

A new job or change in household income can affect what you owe at tax time.

First step

If federal income tax is withheld from your pay, use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator with a recent pay stub. Give your employer a new W-4 if you decide to adjust it.

Source

  • IRS: Tax withholding
10

Check free tax filing options

#

Eligible filers can prepare a federal return through IRS Free File or get help from IRS volunteer programs.

First step

During filing season, start at the IRS free-filing page and check current eligibility, provider rules, and whether a state return is included.

Source

  • IRS: File your taxes for free
11

Check where your deposits are protected

#

FDIC coverage depends on the bank, account type, ownership, and amount; investments are different.

First step

Confirm that your bank is FDIC-insured and, if you have multiple or large accounts, use the FDIC coverage estimator or contact the FDIC.

Source

  • FDIC: Deposit insurance FAQs
12

Get a second opinion on debt

#

A reputable nonprofit credit counselor can review your budget and options when several debts feel unmanageable.

First step

Ask a counselor for free information first. Check fees, credentials, and any proposed payment plan in writing before signing.

Source

  • CFPB: What is credit counseling?
13

Review your Social Security record

#

Your earnings history affects future benefit estimates, and errors are easier to address when found early.

First step

Use your official my Social Security account to review reported earnings and benefit estimates. Follow SSA instructions if an earnings year looks wrong.

Source

  • Social Security Administration: Your Social Security Statement
14

Compare federal student loan payment plans

#

A lower monthly bill can change your total cost and time in repayment; eligibility depends on your loans.

First step

If you have federal student loans, use Federal Student Aid’s Repayment Calculator to compare estimated monthly and total payments. Confirm the final terms with your servicer.

Source

  • Federal Student Aid: Compare repayment plans
