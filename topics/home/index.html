A PRACTICAL GUIDE FOR LIFE IN THE U.S.

Prepare your home for everyday risks and disruption

Preparing a home does not require doing everything at once. Choose one risk you can reduce today, such as checking an alarm or making a simple plan, and use the linked source for details about your home and local conditions.

This page offers general information. Services and rules can vary by state and change over time. Check the sources and ask a qualified professional about your own medical, legal, or financial situation.

15 STEPS

Steps you can take

15 steps to explore. Choose one that fits your situation; each step has a source you can check.

01

Test your smoke alarms

#

An alarm only helps if it works and you can hear it.

First step

Press the test button monthly. Check for alarms inside each bedroom, outside sleeping areas, and on every level. Ask a landlord if installation is needed.

Source

  • U.S. Fire Administration: Smoke alarms
02

Check carbon monoxide alarms

#

Carbon monoxide cannot be seen or smelled.

First step

Install and maintain CO alarms according to the maker’s directions, especially near sleeping areas.

Source

  • U.S. Fire Administration: Carbon monoxide safety
03

Keep generators outside

#

Engine exhaust can cause deadly carbon monoxide poisoning.

First step

Never run a generator inside a home or garage, even with doors open; keep it at least 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents.

Source

  • CDC: Power outage and generator safety
04

Choose two ways out

#

In a home fire, there may be very little time to leave.

First step

Find two exits from each room if possible, agree on a meeting place outside, and practice leaving.

Source

  • U.S. Fire Administration: Home fire escape plans
05

Turn on local emergency alerts

#

Flood, fire, heat, storm, and other risks differ by location.

First step

Keep wireless emergency alerts on and learn how your local officials send evacuation information.

Source

  • FEMA Ready.gov: Disaster planning
06

Make a household contact plan

#

People may be apart when a disaster happens.

First step

Choose a meeting place and an out-of-area contact; include children, pets, and any accessibility or medical needs.

Source

  • FEMA Ready.gov: Disaster planning
07

Build a small emergency kit

#

An outage or evacuation is easier to handle when basics are ready.

First step

Start with water, food, medicines, a flashlight, chargers, and copies of essential information; add what your household needs.

Source

  • FEMA Ready.gov: Disaster planning
08

Test your home for radon

#

Radon has no smell, and levels can be high in any state.

First step

Use an EPA-guided home test or ask a qualified professional; follow EPA advice if the result is elevated.

Source

  • EPA: Radon in homes
09

Verify a rental before sending money

#

Scammers can copy a real listing and collect deposits for a home they cannot rent.

First step

Check the address and owner or manager through independent sources, see the place if you can, and read the lease before paying or sharing sensitive documents.

Source

  • FTC: Rental listing scams
10

Check a tenant report after a denial

#

A rental screening error can block housing or raise your deposit.

First step

If a screening report affected your application, use the notice to request a free copy from the reporting company within 60 days. Dispute any inaccurate or outdated item.

Source

  • CFPB: Rental application denied because of a screening report
11

Plan how you would evacuate

#

A warning may come when roads, transit, or the person who usually drives you are unavailable.

First step

Find local evacuation routes and two possible places to go. If you lack a car or need accessible transport, arrange a backup ride and ask local emergency management about available help.

Source

  • FEMA Ready.gov: Are You Ready? guide
12

Plan for medical needs during an outage

#

Electric medical devices and refrigerated medicines may need a specific backup plan.

First step

Ask your clinician or pharmacist how to handle an extended power loss. Write down safe power, medicine storage, and alternate-location options.

Source

  • FEMA Ready.gov: Power outage information sheet
13

Keep essential records reachable

#

After a disaster, identification, insurance, account, and medical details can be needed to get help.

First step

Make secure copies of key documents and contact numbers. Keep one protected copy outside your home or in password-protected storage you can reach.

Source

  • CFPB: Get prepared before a disaster or emergency strikes
14

Check what your home policy covers

#

Standard home and renters policies may leave flood or earthquake losses uncovered, and deductibles matter.

First step

Read the coverage and deductible pages or call your insurer. Ask about risks where you live and what separate coverage would cost before deciding.

Source

  • CFPB: Get prepared before a disaster or emergency strikes
15

Check your vehicle for open recalls

#

A safety defect may have a free manufacturer repair even if the vehicle drives normally.

First step

If you use a car, enter its VIN at NHTSA’s recall page. Follow any interim safety guidance and contact a dealer for an open recall.

Source

  • NHTSA: Check for vehicle recalls
