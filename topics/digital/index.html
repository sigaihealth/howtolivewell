A PRACTICAL GUIDE FOR LIFE IN THE U.S.

Digital safety steps for accounts, devices, and scams

If an account or message worries you, pause before sharing information or sending money. These steps help you secure accounts, recognize common scams, and find a reporting path when something has gone wrong.

This page offers general information. Services and rules can vary by state and change over time. Check the sources and ask a qualified professional about your own medical, legal, or financial situation.

12 STEPS

Steps you can take

12 steps to explore. Choose one that fits your situation; each step has a source you can check.

01

Replace one reused password

#

A stolen password can unlock every account where you used it again.

First step

Start with your main email account. Give it a long, unique password; a password manager can create and remember one for you.

Source

  • FTC: Protect your personal information
02

Add a second sign-in step

#

A password alone may not stop someone who has stolen it.

First step

Turn on two-factor authentication for email, banking, and other important accounts. Choose an authenticator app or security key when offered.

Source

  • FTC: Use two-factor authentication
03

Turn on automatic updates

#

Security fixes help close gaps that attackers use.

First step

Check update settings on your phone and computer, including the browser and apps you use most.

Sources

  • FTC: Protect your personal information
  • FTC: Protect your phone
04

Lock your phone before it is lost

#

Your phone may hold messages, photos, passwords, and payment apps.

First step

Set an automatic screen lock with a passcode of at least six digits. Turn on the built-in feature that can locate, lock, or erase a lost phone.

Source

  • FTC: Protect your phone
05

Back up what you cannot replace

#

A lost or broken device should not be your only copy of important files.

First step

Turn on a phone or computer backup to a service or drive you control. Check that a recent photo or document appears in the backup.

Sources

  • FTC: Protect your phone
  • FTC: Recognize and avoid phishing
06

Open the app, not the surprise link

#

Fake delivery, password-reset, and account alerts can lead to stolen logins or malware.

First step

If a message asks you to sign in or open an attachment, use the company app or a website you already know. Report and delete the suspicious message.

Source

  • FTC: Recognize and avoid phishing
07

Check your home Wi-Fi settings

#

A router left with default access can expose devices on your network.

First step

If you manage the router, change its default admin password, use WPA2 or WPA3 security, and check for updates. Ask your internet provider for help if it manages the device.

Source

  • FTC: Secure your home Wi-Fi
08

Review what your apps can see

#

Some apps can access your location, contacts, or photos even when they do not need them.

First step

Open your phone’s privacy settings. Turn off permissions an app does not need to work, or remove apps you no longer use.

Source

  • FTC: Website and app privacy
09

Check the site on public Wi-Fi

#

Encryption helps on public networks, but it cannot make a fake website trustworthy.

First step

Before signing in, check the site address and look for HTTPS. Keep your device updated, and leave a site that asks for information you did not expect.

Source

  • FTC: Public Wi-Fi safety
10

Act on a real data-breach notice

#

The useful next step depends on what information was exposed.

First step

Go to the company’s site yourself to confirm the notice. Change any exposed or reused password, then use IdentityTheft.gov/DataBreach for steps matched to the data involved.

Sources

  • FTC: What to do after a data breach
  • FTC: Data breach recovery guide
11

Recover a taken-over account

#

Quick action can limit further access and warn people who may receive fake messages from you.

First step

Use the provider’s own recovery page. Once back in, change the password, sign out other sessions, check recovery details, and alert contacts if messages were sent.

Source

  • FTC: Recover a hacked account
12

Report a scam you spotted

#

The FTC uses scam reports to spot patterns and warn others.

First step

Keep the messages, receipts, and contact details. Report what happened at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If you paid or shared personal information, follow the FTC recovery guide right away.

Sources

  • FTC: ReportFraud
  • FTC: What to do if you were scammed
