A PRACTICAL GUIDE FOR LIFE IN THE U.S.

Family care steps for children, older adults, and caregivers

Caring for someone often means balancing their needs with your own time and resources. Choose one next step for a child, an older adult, a person with a disability, or yourself as a caregiver, and check the linked service for local details.

This page offers general information. Services and rules can vary by state and change over time. Check the sources and ask a qualified professional about your own medical, legal, or financial situation.

24 STEPS

Steps you can take

24 steps to explore. Choose one that fits your situation; each step has a source you can check.

01

Set up a safer place for a baby to sleep

#

A clear, firm, flat sleep space lowers the risk of sleep-related infant death.

First step

Put babies on their backs for every sleep, in their own crib or bassinet with a fitted sheet. Keep pillows, blankets, and toys out.

Source

  • CDC: Safe sleep for babies
02

Name one adult to watch children near water

#

Drowning can happen quickly and quietly, even when a child has taken swim lessons.

First step

At a pool, lake, or bathtub, choose an adult whose only job is close, active supervision; trade off before that adult takes a break.

Source

  • CDC: Preventing drowning
03

Move medicines out of children’s reach

#

Young children can get into medicines, vitamins, and supplements in seconds.

First step

Put them up and away, out of sight, after every use. Ask visitors to secure medicines in their bags too.

Source

  • CDC: Medication safety at home
04

Share a child development concern early

#

Milestone lists help start a conversation; they do not diagnose a child.

First step

Use CDC’s milestone tool, then tell the child’s clinician about any skill that worries you. Ask about screening or early intervention.

Source

  • CDC: Learn the Signs. Act Early.
05

Prepare for a child’s routine checkup

#

A well visit is a chance to discuss growth, school, mood, and prevention before there is a crisis.

First step

Check when the next visit is due. Bring your questions, vaccine record, and any concerns from other caregivers or teachers.

Source

  • HHS MyHealthfinder: Child checkups
06

Make room for a teen to talk

#

A calm, repeated invitation can make it easier to speak about stress, mental health, or substance use.

First step

Ask an open question while doing something together, listen without judgment, and offer to help find support if they want it.

Source

  • CDC: Talk to your teen about mental health
07

Check local child care cost help

#

States and territories run child care assistance and other programs, with different rules.

First step

Use Childcare.gov to find your state or territory’s options, then ask the local program about eligibility and openings.

Source

  • Childcare.gov: Child care financial assistance
08

Look for a nearby Head Start program

#

Head Start and Early Head Start connect young children and families with early learning and other supports.

First step

Search by ZIP code in the official center locator. Ask the local program how to apply and whether space is available.

Source

  • HHS: Head Start Center Locator
09

Ask a local WIC office about food support

#

WIC may offer food, nutrition help, and referrals during pregnancy and early childhood.

First step

Find your state or local WIC agency and ask how to apply. The agency can explain its current eligibility rules.

Source

  • USAGov: WIC food assistance
10

Ask the school about an evaluation

#

A student who is struggling may need an evaluation to see whether disability-related support applies.

First step

Write down your concerns and ask the public school how to request an evaluation. Keep a copy of your request and the response.

Source

  • USAGov: Early intervention and special education services
11

Clear one fall hazard at home

#

Trips at home can cause serious injuries, especially for older adults.

First step

Walk a usual route with the person who uses it. Remove clutter and loose rugs, improve lighting, and note where a grab bar or rail may help.

Source

  • CDC: Preventing older adult falls
12

Add balance and strength to an older adult’s week

#

Balance and strength activity can help reduce fall risk.

First step

Ask a clinician or local senior center about a suitable class or routine, especially after a fall or if walking feels unsteady.

Sources

  • CDC: Preventing older adult falls
  • CDC: Vision health and falls
13

Review an older adult’s medicine list

#

Some medicines and combinations can cause dizziness or other problems.

First step

List prescriptions, over-the-counter drugs, and supplements. Bring the list to a pharmacist or clinician and ask about side effects; do not stop a medicine on your own.

Source

  • NIA: Taking medicines safely as you age
14

Plan rides before driving becomes difficult

#

Having other ways to get around helps an older adult keep appointments and connections.

First step

Together, list nearby transit, rides from trusted people, and delivery options. Discuss any driving concerns with a clinician.

Source

  • CDC: Older adult drivers
15

Talk about future care wishes

#

Loved ones can make better decisions when they know what matters to the person receiving care.

First step

Ask whom the person trusts to speak for them and what care matters most. Ask a care team about advance directives and your state’s forms.

Source

  • NIA: Advance care planning
16

Start one shared care record

#

A clear record makes handoffs and appointments easier for a caregiving team.

First step

With the person’s permission, keep contacts, medicines, appointments, and questions in one place that the right helpers can access.

Source

  • NIA: Caregiver worksheets
17

Split caregiving into specific tasks

#

One person does not have to handle every ride, meal, call, and form.

First step

Ask the person receiving care what help they want. Write a short task list and invite trusted people to take one recurring job each.

Source

  • NIA: Sharing caregiving responsibilities
18

Plan a caregiver break

#

Respite care can give a regular caregiver time to rest or handle other needs.

First step

Ask local aging or disability services about in-home, day, or short-stay respite. Check availability, cost, and fit before booking.

Source

  • NIA: What is respite care?
19

Find local aging services

#

Nearby programs may help with meals, transportation, home support, or caregiver needs.

First step

Search the Eldercare Locator by ZIP code or call its information line, then ask which local services fit your situation.

Source

  • Administration for Community Living: Eldercare Locator
20

Check in on the caregiver too

#

Caregiving can strain sleep, health, and relationships over time.

First step

Ask a caregiver what would lighten this week. Offer one specific errand, meal, ride, or hour of relief they can accept or decline.

Source

  • NIA: Taking care of yourself as a caregiver
21

Ask for disability communication access

#

People with hearing, vision, or speech disabilities may need aids to take part in care or public services.

First step

When scheduling, explain which aid you need, such as a qualified sign-language interpreter, captions, or an accessible document. Ask how it will be provided.

Source

  • U.S. Department of Justice: ADA effective communication
22

Ask about accessible transit options

#

A disability can make standard bus or rail service hard to use for some trips.

First step

Contact your local transit agency about accessible routes and ADA paratransit. Ask how its eligibility process works; a diagnosis alone does not decide it.

Source

  • Federal Transit Administration: ADA paratransit questions
23

Plan for disability needs in an emergency

#

A power outage or evacuation can interrupt equipment, medicines, and daily support.

First step

With the person involved, list essential supplies, helpers, accessible transport, and backup power needs. Share the plan with trusted people.

Source

  • FEMA Ready.gov: Emergency planning with a disability
24

Explore disability services by need

#

Support may involve health care, transportation, work, school, or financial programs.

First step

Use USAGov’s disability directory to choose the need that matters now, then check the program’s own application rules.

Source

  • USAGov: Disability services
